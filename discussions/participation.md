# Discussion participation — what it is, and how it is graded

Nine discussions across the semester. This page tells you what happens in the room, what you submit
afterwards, and how it counts.

## What a discussion session looks like

Roughly 30 minutes inside a normal 75-minute class, run as **think–pair–share**:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Before class** | Read the assigned source (usually one, under 30 minutes) and the background section of that discussion's packet. Both are posted on Brightspace a week ahead. |
| **~5 min** | Instructor sets up the case — what happened, what the investigation found, where the disagreement is. |
| **~6 min** | **Think.** You write, alone, on an index card: one sentence answering the assigned question, and a confidence number from 0 to 10. |
| **~8 min** | **Pair.** You and one partner argue it out. The pair produces one sentence you will both sign — or, if you cannot agree, the single piece of evidence that would change one of your minds. |
| **~8 min** | **Share.** We take it to the room. Expect to be asked to defend a position you do not hold. |
| **~2 min** | Close. Usually without resolution. That is deliberate. |

Cards are collected. Several discussions get re-asked later in the semester so you can see how the
room moved — and how you moved.

## What you submit

**One paragraph, 150–250 words, due 11:59 p.m. two days after the session**, to that discussion's
Brightspace assignment folder.

Answer three things:

1. **Where did you land, and why?** State a position. "Both sides have a point" is not a position.
2. **What was the strongest argument against you?** Name it specifically, and say why it did not
   move you — or say that it did.
3. **What would change your mind?** Name a fact, a study, a ruling, or an outcome. If nothing
   would, say that, and say what that implies about whether this is an empirical question at all.

Write it yourself, after class, in one sitting. It should take twenty minutes.

## How it is graded

Each reflection is scored **0–2**:

| Score | Means |
|---|---|
| **2** | Takes a real position. Engages the strongest opposing argument specifically, not a caricature of it. Names something falsifiable that would change your mind. Shows you did the reading and were in the room. |
| **1** | Present and on topic, but hedged, generic, restates the framing without adding anything, or answers only one of the three questions. |
| **0** | Missing, off topic, or plainly written without doing the reading or attending. |

Nine reflections, **lowest score dropped**, so eight count for a maximum of 16 points.

### Where this sits in the course grade

Participation is 20% of the course, split:

| Component | Share of course grade |
|---|---|
| Discussion reflections (9, lowest dropped) | 10% |
| Leading one in-class case study | 5% |
| In-class engagement and peer feedback on project presentations | 5% |

### The two things that reliably cost points

**Hedging.** A reflection that carefully declines to conclude anything reads as safe and scores a 1.
You are not graded on holding the correct view — several of these questions do not have one that I
hold confidently — you are graded on whether you took a position and tested it.

**Strawmanning.** If you characterize the opposing view in a way its actual proponents would not
recognize, you have not engaged it. The readings deliberately include sources that cut against
each discussion's framing, precisely so that the strongest opposing argument is available to you.

### On changing your mind

Saying "I came in thinking X, and reading Y moved me to Z" is a **2**, not a weakness. Saying
"I came in thinking X and nothing moved me, and here is the specific evidence that would have"
is also a 2. What scores badly is having no position to move.

## AI use

Allowed, disclosed, and — as everywhere in this course — your responsibility. Two practical notes:

- A reflection generated wholesale by an LLM is obvious to read and scores a 1 at best, because it
  will hedge, it will not reference what was actually said in the room, and it will not name a
  falsifier. That is not a detection claim; it is a description of what the output looks like.
- Using an assistant to argue *against* you before you write — "give me the strongest case that I
  am wrong" — is a genuinely good use of it, and one I would encourage. Say so in your disclosure.

## Missing a session

The lowest score is dropped, which covers one absence with no explanation needed. If you must miss
more, tell me in advance and submit the reflection anyway based on the reading — you will lose the
in-room component but keep the credit.

## Accommodations

If speaking in a group is a barrier for you, come talk to me in the first two weeks. There is more
than one way to demonstrate engagement, and I would rather design around it than have you dread a
third of the sessions. Accommodations arranged through
[Student Access](https://www.vanderbilt.edu/student-access/) are applied automatically.
